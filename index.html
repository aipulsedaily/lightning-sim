What this is

A lightning flash, simulated from the electrostatics up. Nothing here is drawn along a path chosen in advance: the channel is grown one step at a time by a stochastic rule whose only input is the local electric field, and everything that follows — where it strikes, how much current it carries, how hot it gets, what it sounds like — comes out of that channel rather than being imposed on it.

The sequence

  1. Initiation. A thundercloud carries three stacked charge regions: positive above, a large negative layer on the −10 °C to −25 °C isotherms, and a smaller positive one near the base. The flash begins wherever the field between them first beats the local breakdown threshold, which falls with air density — so higher is easier, and that is a large part of why three quarters of all flashes never leave the cloud.
  2. The bidirectional leader. The channel is an isolated conductor, so it must stay electrically neutral, and its potential floats to the average of its surroundings. This is Kasemir's insight from 1960, and it explains the opening act: the positive end (which needs a weaker field) climbs into the negative charge region first, dragging the whole channel's potential down with it, and only then does the negative end find itself tens of megavolts below its surroundings and start driving for the ground.
  3. The stepped leader. It advances in steps of tens of metres at around 2×105 m/s, branching downward, laying a few coulombs of negative charge along fifteen kilometres of channel. Barely visible to the eye; obvious on a high-speed camera.
  4. Attachment. Within a striking distance of about 10 I0.65 metres, grounded objects launch upward connecting leaders. Whichever gets one away first collects the stroke, which is why a mast protects the field around it.
  5. The return stroke. The channel is shorted to earth, and a neutralisation wave runs back up it at roughly a third of the speed of light. Thirty kiloamps, thirty thousand kelvin, a hundred microseconds. Tens of milliseconds to charge, tens of microseconds to discharge: that ratio is why lightning is violent.
  6. And again. After about 60 ms of darkness a dart leader retraces the still-warm channel a hundred times faster than the stepped leader did, and fires another stroke. Three to five is typical. In a third of flashes a continuing current of 100–200 amps then flows for up to half a second — the part that actually starts fires.

Thunder

Every metre of channel is its own sound source. The shock wave from each expands to a relaxation radius of a few metres and decays into an N-wave a few milliseconds long, which is why thunder peaks near 50–100 Hz. Because the channel is kilometres long, its sound arrives spread over many seconds in the order the geometry dictates: the crack is the near, nearly equidistant part, and the rumble is everything else. The simulation builds a real acoustic impulse response from the channel it grew and convolves through it, so counting the seconds after the flash gives the right distance.

Your own photographs

Drop an image onto the window and it is unprojected into a 3-D scene the lightning stands inside, then click anywhere to move the storm there. The horizon is found automatically — it fixes the camera's pitch, and everything else is measured from it — and rays below it are dropped onto the ground plane while rays above meet the cloud deck, which is what puts the reconstruction in metres rather than in arbitrary units. A depth network can be switched on to add real relief to the foreground; it is fetched only if you ask for it, and the geometry stays underneath because that is what gives the network's output a scale.

It works best on the kind of photograph you would want lightning in: horizon visible, sky above, ground or water below. Close subjects and interiors have no horizon to find, and the panel will say so.
